
\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:analysis}

\subsection{Why Does A3 Work?}

A3's success comes from three ideas working in tandem:

\subsubsection{Fisher Weighting Reduces Effective Rank}

In transformer layers, activation variance is extremely non-uniform. 
\textbf{Figure 8.1} shows the sorted Fisher diagonal for a middle DistilBERT layer. 
The top 10\% of channels account for \textbf{85\%} of total energy; 
the bottom 50\% contribute less than \textbf{2\%}.

By weighting the input space by $\sqrt{F}$, A3 effectively compresses only 
the important directions.

\subsubsection{Adaptive Rank Handles Layer Heterogeneity}

Different layers have different intrinsic rank. \textbf{Figure 8.2} shows the 
final chosen rank per layer. Early and late layers tend to be more 
compressible; middle layers require higher rank.

\subsubsection{LN Recalibration Corrects Distributional Shift}

After factorization, activation distributions shift. Without recalibration, 
the standard deviation across features changes by up to \textbf{15\%} 
(measured empirically). LN recalibration brings this back to within 
\textbf{3\%}.

\subsection{Spectral Analysis}

\textbf{Figure 8.3} shows singular value decay for representative layers.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Attention layers:} Rapid decay (effective rank ~48)
\item \textbf{FFN layers:} Slower decay (effective rank ~192)

\end{itemize}
This justifies the per-layer rank selection.

\subsection{Numerical Stability}

\subsubsection{FP16 vs FP32 SVD}

We compute SVD in FP32 for stability. FP16 SVD occasionally produces 
NaN for ill-conditioned matrices.

\subsubsection{Weighting Magnitude}

The weighted weight matrix $W_\text{weighted} = W \cdot \mathrm{diag}(\sqrt{F})$ 
can have very different magnitudes across dimensions. We clamp $\sqrt{F}$ to 
$[10^{-3}, 10^3]$ to prevent overflow.

\subsubsection{Residual Analysis}

\textbf{Figure 8.4} shows $W - UV$ residual norm across layers. Residuals are 
concentrated in low-energy dimensions, which is exactly what Fisher weighting 
is designed to allow.

\subsubsection{Underlying Causes}

Empirical spectral profiling reveals two distinct structural phenomena explaining why these layers resisted low-rank compression within the $\tau = 0.40$ budget:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Value and Output Projection Entropy:} Unlike query and key projections which primarily perform subspace alignment for dot-product attention scores, value ($V$) and output ($O$) projections directly transform and mix token feature representations across all 768 dimensions. Consequently, their singular value spectra decay much more slowly (effective rank $\sim 192$--$256$), resulting in higher relative Frobenius error ($0.45$--$0.55$) when bounded by $r_{\mathrm{ceiling}} \le 256$.
    \item \textbf{Deep Layer Dimensionality:} As sequence representations progress into deeper layers (Layers 4 and 5), contextual interactions introduce higher semantic complexity, broadening activation variance across higher-order singular vectors. The safety fallback mechanism in A3 detects this boundary condition and preserves the original dense weights, maintaining 90.02\% accuracy without degrading task performance.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Fisher Diagonal in Practice}

\textbf{Figure 8.5} visualizes the empirical Fisher diagonal spectrum $F$ across layer depths:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Layer 0 (Early):} Highly concentrated energy profile where the top 10\% of feature coordinates account for over 85\% of the total sensitivity norm.
\item \textbf{Layer 3 (Middle):} Moderate concentration with balanced diagonal variance across intermediate attention channels.
\item \textbf{Layer 5 (Late):} Diffuse sensitivity distribution reflecting contextualized multi-token feature mixing.
\end{itemize}

Early layers tend to have \textbf{highly concentrated} energy in a few channels; 
later layers have \textbf{more uniform} distributions. This explains why query and key projections in early layers compress with substantially lower Frobenius reconstruction error.

\subsection{Recovery Dynamics}

\textbf{Figure 8.6} shows LoRA recovery curves during parameter adaptation. For DistilBERT, cross-entropy loss decreases monotonically from $0.0083$ down to $0.0008$ by step 50. For RoBERTa, loss stabilizes rapidly near $0.0012$, with rank-16 adapters successfully restoring full classification margin within 25 optimization steps.

\textbf{Observation.} Recovery fine-tuning is lightweight yet critical: 50 gradient steps on rank-16 adapters close over 85\% of the initial post-factorization accuracy gap without updating frozen factorized bases.

\subsection{Summary of Insights}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Fisher weighting is essential:} Without it, error increases by
  2.5\%.
\item \textbf{LN recalibration helps:} It closes ~60\% of the recovery gap.
\item \textbf{Adaptive rank is necessary:} Fixed rank yields high error on some layers.
\item \textbf{Calibration is cheap:} 1000 examples suffice.
\item \textbf{FFN is more compressible than attention:} FFN effective rank is
  192 vs attention's 48.
\end{enumerate}
